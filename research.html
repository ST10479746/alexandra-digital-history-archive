A guide for curious readers

Ask more of a source.

Who made it, and why?

Record the author or institution, the date and the intended audience. Ask whether you are reading a speech, a recollection, a transcript or a later explanation.

Separate the date of the event from the date the account was created. A memory written decades later offers a different perspective from a contemporary record.

What supports the claim?

Follow references to the original material where possible. Compare the same event across independent accounts, and note disagreements rather than silently removing them.

A primary source is valuable evidence of its author’s perspective. It still needs interpretation and does not automatically settle every historical question.

How should an image or recording be described?

Record the creator, date, location, source and permission or licence where known. Distinguish a historical record from an illustration or a modern reconstruction.

Generated images and synthetic voices should be identified clearly. They cannot verify a historical event or stand in for an authentic resident’s testimony.

What belongs in a reference?

Keep the author or organisation, year, full title, publisher or website, URL and access date together. Use the referencing style required by your institution.

Example: Phahle, R. (2019). The 1957 Alexandra Bus Boycott and its unsung heroes. South African History Online. Available at the source link above (Accessed: 25 October 2026).

Can I reuse material from a linked website?

A source link credits research; it does not grant permission to copy the source’s images, recordings or full text. Read the rights information and obtain permission where required.

This directory links to the original publishers. Their images and documents have not been copied into this resource list.
